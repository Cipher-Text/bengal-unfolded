# Bengal Unfolded: Content and Performance Analysis

Prepared 2026-08-28 from the repository corpus and the analytics evidence stored in `docs/SEO_GSC_OPTIMIZATION_LOG.md`.

## Executive assessment

Bengal Unfolded already has a strong, source-oriented Bangladesh/Bengal historical spine: 168 event chapters, 551 figure records, 552 shared resources, 60 places, 17 topic hubs, and 34 glossary entries. Its distinctive identity is not a general “facts” page. It is a bilingual, evidence-first history project that explains how political change, territory, memory, state power, and everyday life became connected.

The archive is strongest from the Bengal Sultanate through colonial rule, 1947, 1971, and post-1971 Bangladesh. It is especially rich in political transitions, liberation-war chapters, civic movements, and contested modern history. The next editorial cycle should widen the lens without abandoning that core: more trade, river and city history; more South Asian peoples and borderlands; more women, workers, artisans, and minority/community perspectives; and a selective global comparison series on divided or stateless peoples.

The largest practical constraint is measurement: no Facebook/Meta export, GA export, or raw GSC CSV was found in the repository. Therefore, no defensible claim can be made here about Facebook reach, shares, reactions, comments, follower conversion, format performance, or posting time. The plan uses the available GSC evidence for search potential and the existing content corpus to avoid near-duplicates; all Facebook performance hypotheses should be tested with a real post export.

## Existing coverage and repetition risk

### Already-covered clusters

- **Ancient and early Bengal:** Wari-Bateshwar, Mahasthangarh, Gangaridai context, Shashanka, Pala-Sena transitions, Mainamati, Somapura.
- **Sultanate and regional power:** Bengal’s independence and unification, Ilyas Shah, Raja Ganesha/Jalaluddin, Husain Shahi rule, Sylhet, Bagerhat, Gaur, and the transition to Mughal power.
- **Mughal Bengal:** Dhaka/Jahangirnagar, Shaista Khan, Chittagong, Baro-Bhuyans, Portuguese Hooghly, revenue pressure, Rajmahal, and the nawabi transition.
- **Company and colonial rule:** Plassey, Buxar, Diwani, the 1770 famine, Permanent Settlement, Fakir-Sannyasi resistance, Rangpur, Faraizi, Titumir, Indigo, Pabna, Santal, 1857, education, representation, and the Bengal Tenancy Act.
- **Late colonial Bengal:** 1905 partition, Swadeshi, Muslim League’s Dhaka founding, separate electorates, annulment, Lucknow Pact, Khilafat, Dhaka University, Bengal Pact, Chittagong Armoury Raid, 1937 politics, Lahore Resolution, 1943 famine, Tebhaga, Direct Action Day, Noakhali, and United Bengal proposals.
- **Partition and East Pakistan:** Radcliffe Award, Sylhet referendum, Eastern Bengal, language politics, United Front, constitutional representation, martial law, education movement, Hazratbal tensions, 1964 riots, 1965 war, Six Points, Agartala, 1969, and Bhola/1970 election.
- **1971:** Declaration and proclamation, Mujibnagar, Operation Searchlight, riverine operations, Operation Jackpot, multiple fronts and battles, massacres, intellectual killings, surrender, and memory/resources.
- **Post-1971 Bangladesh:** Constitution, famine, Special Powers Act, BAKSAL, August 1975, jail killing, November 1975, Zia, Ershad, 1990, parliamentary return, Farakka, CHT conflict and accord, caretaker politics, 2001 border clash, emergency rule, Pilkhana, tribunal, Shahbag, communal violence, garment disasters, elections, digital law, Rohingya influx, and 2024 movements.

### Topics to avoid repeating soon

The next 60 should not use the familiar headline angle for Plassey, the 1905 partition, the Language Movement, the broad 1971 war, March 7, Shahbag, 1990, Ershad, the Bhola cyclone, the Radcliffe Line, or the Rohingya influx. These subjects may return only through a materially different lens—merchant finance rather than battlefield drama, a woman’s archive rather than a movement summary, a river or city rather than a national overview, or a carefully sourced comparison.

The plan also avoids making another generic “history of the Mughal Empire,” “history of Bangladesh,” or “Kashmir conflict” post. Where an existing event already covers the core event, the new idea is framed as a mechanism, person, place, economic system, or cross-border consequence.

## What the data says Bengal Unfolded should do next

### Available search evidence

The maintained GSC log records two baselines:

- Apr 29–Jun 27, 2026: 28,710 impressions, 87 clicks, 0.30% CTR, average position 11.43.
- May 18–Aug 17, 2026: 47,758 impressions, 163 clicks, approximately 0.34% CTR, average position approximately 11.4.

Compared with the first baseline, impressions rose about 66% and clicks about 87%, while CTR remained low and broadly flat. The defensible conclusion is that discoverability/indexing is improving faster than search-result click conversion. The log also identifies high-impression, zero-click figure pages (Mirza Mughal, Mirza Khizr Sultan, Mirza Abu Bakr) and a comparatively successful Bangla 1947 partition event page (9 clicks from 599 impressions, average position 7.95). This supports distinctive, answer-first headlines and deeper internal linking, not simply more volume.

### Implications for Facebook publishing

No local file measures Facebook performance, so the following are testable recommendations rather than measured findings:

- **Strongest likely editorial territory:** Bengal’s borderlands, city changes, economic systems, overlooked actors, and “one decision changed a map” stories. These naturally combine a clear question with a strong visual.
- **Strongest evidenced search hooks:** specific people and specific mechanisms. The GSC log shows visibility for named Mughal figures and for 1947 partition intent, but weak click conversion when the page promise is generic. Use a concrete fact in the Facebook headline and first sentence.
- **Recommended formats to test:** map explainer, archive/photo-led story, myth-versus-history, timeline, and economic explainer. Do not assume carousel superiority; compare each against a single-image 4:5 post with the same editorial promise.
- **Reach versus meaningful response:** once Meta data is available, rank posts on shares/impressions and interactions/impressions, not reach alone. A low-reach post that earns discussion or sharing may be a better model than a high-reach generic post.
- **Series:** use recurring labels—“মানচিত্রের পেছনের ইতিহাস,” “ভুলে যাওয়া বাংলা,” “ইতিহাসের অর্থনীতি,” “যে শহর একসময়…,” and “বিভক্ত জাতি”—as recognition devices, but vary the visual and narrative treatment.
- **Frequency:** a sustainable starting test is 4 posts per week for 15 weeks, with one flexible slot for a timely anniversary or archive discovery. Record date, local time, format, topic, reach, impressions, reactions, comments, shares, follows, and link clicks for every post.
- **CTA:** end with one answerable historical question, not a generic “what do you think?” Add a soft follow CTA only after high-value posts: “এই ধারাবাহিকের পরের মানচিত্রটি দেখতে Bengal Unfolded অনুসরণ করুন।”
- **Follower growth:** make the relationship between posts explicit. Link a new post to one older chapter and promise the next related story. This turns isolated virality into a reading path.

### Measurement setup for the next cycle

Export Meta post-level data after every 15 posts. Add columns for content pillar, series, format, visual type, hook type, word count, link/no-link, publish weekday/time, reach, impressions, 3-second/video views if relevant, reactions, comments, shares, saves, link clicks, and follows. Calculate:

`interactions = reactions + comments + shares + saves`

`interaction rate = interactions / impressions`

`share rate = shares / impressions`

`follow conversion = follows / reach`

Compare medians by pillar and format, and retain a separate “high efficiency / low reach” list. Do not make strategic decisions from raw reach alone.

## Editorial gaps this plan addresses

1. **Economic history:** money, insurance, merchant finance, river ports, jute, tea, salt, textiles, taxation, and labour are underrepresented compared with political chronology.
2. **Geographic breadth:** the archive has key places but the social meaning of river change, hill frontiers, chars, ports, and border corridors needs more public-facing treatment.
3. **People beyond political elites:** women, artisans, weavers, boatmen, plantation workers, students, refugees, minority communities, and merchant networks need stronger narrative presence.
4. **South Asian peoples and internal diversity:** existing coverage is Bengal-heavy; there is room for Assamese, Chakma, Naga, Manipuri, Baloch, Sindhi, Kashmiri, Tamil, Pashtun, Rohingya, and Gorkha stories handled without stereotyping.
5. **Empire as a system:** the next cycle should explain how Maurya, Gupta, Delhi Sultanate, Mughal, Maratha, Sikh, Company, and princely-state systems governed distance, revenue, mobility, and difference.
6. **Global comparisons:** a selective set of Kurdish, Palestinian, Armenian, Basque, Sámi, Amazigh, Uyghur, Māori, and Roma stories can extend the page’s core question—how identities survive, change, or become divided by states—without turning the feed into a survey course.
7. **Religious and cultural plurality:** Sufi networks, Bhakti, Buddhist institutions, reform movements, printing, translation, foodways, and shared sacred landscapes deserve more treatment beyond political labels.
